    Best practices

    A pattern catalogue for scene authors. Three top-level sections:

    1. Control design — how to lay out a scene's author-facing knobs so Perform, Snap, presets, and playlists stay composable.
    2. GLSL pitfalls — common foot-guns when writing shaders against the chroma_* uniform surface.
    3. GLSL performance — cost considerations, when to reach for Lygia, and the honest picture of what the V1 preview adapter does and doesn't render.

    Read top-down on the first pass; each subsection is a named pattern or pitfall and is independently citable. If you only have five minutes, the four most leverage-y items are Control surface composability, Coerce booleans through > 0.5 (§2.2), Branchless over divergent branches, and The canvas-2d preview is not your scene.

    1 — Control design

    A scene's CONTROLS array is the public API your scene exposes to operators, snaps, presets, OSC, MIDI, and the in-app preview adapter all at once. The fields are documented in Scene Workflow; this section covers what to do with them.

    1.1 Pick the smallest control surface that exposes intent

    Every control you declare becomes:

    • a row in Perform's Scene Controls panel,
    • a slot in every preset captured against this scene,
    • a value persisted into every Snap,
    • a route under /chroma/control/<name> for OSC operators,
    • a learnable target for MIDI bindings.

    That's five surfaces multiplied by the count. Five controls is fine. Twenty is operator-hostile. Thirty is unusable in Perform.

    A useful sieve: if two controls always move together in your own testing, fold them into one. A tint colour with a per-channel tint_r / tint_g / tint_b triplet adjacent to it is one control, not four. A position xy plus a separate position_x and position_y is one xy control, not three.

    1.2 Name controls for the operator, not the shader

    The NAME field is what the operator reads at perform-time. Two rules that age well:

    • Pick a short verb-or-noun phrase, lower-case, snake-cased. bass_pulse, palette_hue, tunnel_speed. Avoid GLSL-internal spellings like u_pulse_amp_norm.
    • Use the DESCRIPTION field for the longer story. Perform surfaces NAME as the row label; the description shows up in tooltips and in the operator's Help panel. There is no character budget on DESCRIPTION; treat it like a one-sentence operator-facing comment.

    The LABEL legacy field is preserved for ingest of reference scenes but does not need to be authored on new scenes; NAME carries.

    1.3 Snap-friendliness and the control surface as a state vector

    The Snap system captures the full vector of every control's current value at the moment you save the slot. Recalling a slot writes every captured value back. Presets behave the same way under Wave 2.4 PERF.C semantics: Cmd-click captures, click recalls. Playlists transition between presets across scene boundaries.

    The implication: every author-facing control is part of the scene's state vector. Two anti-patterns follow:

    • Don't include "moment-of-author" controls that you don't want operators to capture into Snaps. A debug show_uv toggle, a one- shot randomize_seed bang, a developer-only wireframe flag: drop these before publishing or move them behind your authoring branch.
    • Don't bake operator-facing logic into uniforms the scene hot-reloads under. A control that takes effect only on scene reload is silently broken under Snap recall — the recalled value will not match the visible state. Make controls react every frame, not at boot.

    1.4 Use UI_GROUP to keep Perform legible

    Controls without a UI_GROUP land in the default panel and stack top-to-bottom in declaration order. For scenes with more than ~6 controls, declare an explicit UI_GROUP per cluster. Perform respects the groups as collapsible panels under Scene Controls; the Library scene-detail pane shows the group taxonomy too.

    A workable convention:

    • appearance — colours, palettes, tint, alpha.
    • motion — speed, rotation, warp, drift.
    • audio — per-band gains, reactivity multipliers.
    • geometry — scale, position, count, density.
    • meta-output — the four canonical IS_META flags (mirror, invert, global tint, master gain).
    • meta-pipeline — anything else that affects the global pipeline rather than the scene's intrinsic look.

    The strings are operator-facing prose; pick what reads cleanly in your scene's domain. Stay consistent across scenes if you ship a library.

    1.5 The IS_META flag and the _Reactivity meta-gate

    IS_META: true on a control signals "this is a global pipeline adjustment, not an intrinsic scene parameter." The fixture set in engine/scene/tests/fixtures/scenes/meta-controls.chroma enumerates the canonical four:

    • meta_mirror (toggle)
    • meta_invert (toggle)
    • meta_tint (color)
    • meta_gain (slider, [0, 2])

    Beyond those four, a separate first-class meta-control — _Reactivity — multiplies every audio uniform globally before the shader reads it. It's part of the Advanced edition meta-gate per the catalog and is what gives operators a single knob to turn down audio response without rewriting every shader's mix factor.

    Pattern. Author your shader against the raw audio uniforms; reach for an IS_META audio_gain slider local to your scene only if you need scene-specific trim distinct from the global _Reactivity knob. Otherwise leave the global meta-knob to do its job.

    1.6 Range, default, and the "interesting zone"

    Three patterns that pay for themselves:

    • Set MIN and MAX to the range you actually want the operator to explore. A slider [0, 1000] where every interesting value lives in [0, 10] wastes the operator's fine motor control.
    • Pick DEFAULT at the most-compelling-looking sample of the range, not the midpoint and not zero. The operator's first encounter with your scene is the default. If 0.0 is dead-silent black, that's a bad first impression.
    • Avoid exact-zero defaults on multiplicative axes. A gain or intensity slider that starts at 0.0 makes the scene look broken at load time. Start at 0.5 and let the operator dial down.

    1.7 Curves: smooth variants, knobs vs sliders

    The TYPE field accepts the base kind plus a smoothing variant after the first whitespace token (slider smooth, bang smooth, xy smooth). The base kind drives the schema; the variant drives the runtime smoothing behaviour. Specifically:

    • slider vs knob — the engine treats both as scalar inputs through the same pipeline. Pick knob when the visual affordance in Perform should be rotary (small parameter, fits the dense scene-controls grid), slider when linear travel reads more intuitive (gains, levels, anything 0-to-1).
    • slider smooth etc. — apply the smoothing variant to any control the operator will sweep aggressively. The smoothing removes step-jaggies when the value is bound to MIDI faders or swept via OSC at high update rate.
    • bang vs bang smooth — momentary triggers. Smoothing on bang decays the trigger toward zero with a configurable half-life. Without smoothing, the trigger fires for exactly one frame.

    The PARAMS field on a control carries variant-specific smoothing payload (target half-life, smoothing window, etc.) when the variant uses one.

    1.8 How the preview adapter consumes named controls

    The Perform / Library / Author preview tiles render through the canvas-2d procedural adapter (SceneCanvas.svelte), not through a GPU GLSL pipeline. The preview is wireframe-faithful for procedural shapes but is not rasterising your scene's actual shader. The ADR-011 GPU pipeline lands in Phase-2+; until then, the preview adapter reads three named pre-multiplier control names verbatim:

    • speed (slider in [0, 1], nominal 0.5) pre-multiplies the renderer's time argument. 0.0 freezes the preview, 1.0 runs at 2x.
    • intensity (slider in [0, 1], nominal 0.5) pre-multiplies the renderer's audio-level argument with the same 2x scaling.
    • tint (vec3 or vec4 in [0, 1] per channel) draws a translucent overlay over the procedural vignette at 20% of the supplied alpha.

    Plus four control-coverage extras: invert, mirror, hor_mirror / horizontal_mirror, vert_mirror / vertical_mirror. These render as post-pass overlays at the canvas-2d adapter level.

    Pattern. If you want your scene's preview tile to "do the right thing" without operators having to imagine the GPU render, name your top-level speed / intensity / tint controls exactly those three spellings. The preview adapter will respect them. Other controls still dispatch through the runtime command bus to the engine — they're just not visualised in the canvas-2d preview.

    2 — GLSL pitfalls

    This section catalogues mistakes that compile cleanly but produce silently-wrong visuals at runtime. Read Chromaticity Uniforms for the canonical uniform vocabulary before reaching here for debugging.

    2.1 A typo at the uniform declaration site silently binds zero

    The runtime does not perform per-binding casing fixups. If your shader declares uniform float chroma_audiorms; (lowercase r) instead of chroma_AudioRMS, the binding adapter has no symbol to publish into. The compile is clean. The uniform reads zero forever. The scene renders dark.

    Pattern. Copy uniform names from the Chromaticity Uniforms reference verbatim. The scene loader surfaces a warning at load time when a declared uniform name fails to resolve against the catalog; check the load diagnostics on every fresh declaration. Don't rely on visual inspection — a uniform binding zero often looks like the scene "just needs more audio."

    2.2 Coerce toggles through > 0.5, not == 1.0

    GLSL toggles arrive as float uniforms (0.0 or 1.0 on disk). The trip from operator → IPC → JSON → uniform → shader passes through several float coercions; a value that started life as 1.0 may arrive as 0.9999937 by the time the shader reads it. Comparing for strict equality against 1.0 produces sporadic flickering when the toggle is "on but reads slightly less than perfect."

    // FRAGILE — equality against a coerced float
if (u_invert == 1.0) {
    col = vec3(1.0) - col;
}

// ROBUST — half-range threshold
if (u_invert > 0.5) {
    col = vec3(1.0) - col;
}

    The same rule applies to if (audio_band == 0.0) checks; use < 1e-6 or similar. CPU integer comparisons are not GPU float comparisons; thinking in epsilons is the discipline.

    2.3 Integer-vs-float coercion at uniform boundaries

    GLSL implicitly promotes integer literals in expressions, but the rules surprise people. A common pitfall:

    // WRONG — integer division, returns 0
float t = 1 / 2;

// RIGHT — float division, returns 0.5
float t = 1.0 / 2.0;

    This bites authors who copy patterns from CPU languages where literal integer division promotes. In GLSL, 1 / 2 is 0, full stop, and many downstream errors look like "the scene is dark" or "the colours desaturated."

    Similarly, when sampling chroma_AudioFFT[i], declare i as int explicitly:

    int idx = int(floor(t * 8.0));
idx = clamp(idx, 0, 7);
float band = chroma_AudioFFT[idx];

    A float-indexed array access compiles on some GLSL versions and fails on others; the int cast is portable.

    2.4 Out-of-bounds array sampling

    chroma_AudioFFT[8] has exactly eight bands, indices 0..7. Reading chroma_AudioFFT[8] (the off-by-one) is undefined behaviour: some drivers return 0, some return the next memory cell, some kernel- panic the GPU on tiles where unused registers carry old fragment state. Always clamp(idx, 0, 7) before indexing.

    Sampler textures (chroma_Spectrum, chroma_LevelTrail) have the same constraint: the sampler API clamps automatically at the GL level, but the u coordinate you feed needs to be in [0, 1] for documented behaviour. Wrapping mode is sampler-default GL_CLAMP_TO_EDGE unless you opt into something else.

    2.5 The all-zero baseline is real

    Every chroma_* audio uniform reads zero during the no-audio-yet startup window. The first audio tick arrives some tens of milliseconds after the scene loads. A shader that divides by chroma_AudioRMS without an epsilon guard produces inf / NaN / black-screen on the first frame.

    // FRAGILE — division by zero on first frame
vec3 col = base / chroma_AudioRMS;

// ROBUST — saturating fallback
float rms = max(chroma_AudioRMS, 1e-3);
vec3 col = base / rms;

    The same pattern applies to chroma_AudioPeak, chroma_Level, chroma_BPMConfidence, and any time-accelerator (chroma_BassTime etc.) that decays toward zero on silence.

    2.6 Alpha pre-multiplication caveats

    Chromaticity scenes write to a 32-bit RGBA target. The compositor expects non-pre-multiplied alpha by default — i.e. your gl_FragColor carries the colour you want at full opacity and the alpha channel carries the blend factor. Authors who copy a pattern that pre-multiplies (multiplying RGB by A inside the shader) will see their scene appear dim when alpha is below 1.0.

    // NON-PREMULTIPLIED (default)
gl_FragColor = vec4(scene_rgb, scene_alpha);

// PRE-MULTIPLIED (rare; use only with a matching blend mode)
gl_FragColor = vec4(scene_rgb * scene_alpha, scene_alpha);

    If you need pre-multiplied (compositing-over with HDR sources, post-FX that operates in linear-light), document it in the scene's DESCRIPTION field so the operator knows what they're consuming.

    2.7 gl_FragCoord is in pixels, not normalised

    A new author's first instinct is often:

    // WRONG — gl_FragCoord goes 0..width / 0..height, not 0..1
vec2 uv = gl_FragCoord.xy;

    You want one of:

    // Normalised, anisotropic — uv ranges 0..1 in each axis
vec2 uv = gl_FragCoord.xy / iResolution.xy;

// Normalised, aspect-corrected — centre at (0,0), keep y in [-0.5, 0.5]
vec2 uv = (gl_FragCoord.xy - 0.5 * iResolution.xy) / iResolution.y;

    The aspect-corrected form is what audio-reactivity.md's worked example uses; it is the canonical shape for scenes that draw centred radial geometry.

    2.8 Hot-reload preserves chroma_time, not your accumulators

    Live-reload (docs/37) preserves the operator's control values across the reload by re-applying the captured PreservedState. It does not preserve in-shader-state like a feedback accumulator in a multipass FLOAT target — that target gets cleared and your post-reload first frame reads zero.

    Pattern. Don't write logic that depends on the previous frame's contents surviving a hot-reload. If your scene needs warm-up state, seed it from a stable noise function of chroma_time or chroma_AudioTime so the same pattern reproduces on every reload.

    3 — GLSL performance

    A 60 Hz render budget gives you ~16.67 ms per frame for everything: geometry, multipass composition, post-FX, plus the scheduler tick that drains audio and IPC. On a mid-range integrated GPU, that budget is generous for most scenes and tight for the worst-case ones. This section catalogues where cost actually accumulates.

    3.1 Texture sampling is the largest single per-fragment cost

    A modern GPU's texture sampler is fast but not free. A shader that does eight texture() calls per fragment will spend most of its budget in the sampler. The canonical mitigations:

    • Sample once, reuse. If you need chroma_AudioFFT[3] twice in your shader, store the first read into a local float and reuse. The compiler may CSE for you on simple cases, but for sampler reads through chroma_Spectrum it almost certainly will not.
    • Prefer the array uniform over the sampler. Reading chroma_AudioFFT[i] is a uniform fetch, not a sampler read. Reading texture(chroma_Spectrum, u).r is a sampler read. Use the array when you only need the eight-band shape; use the sampler when you need the full 256-or-512-bin spectrum.
    • Avoid texture sampling inside loops with variable bounds. GPU drivers can hoist loop-invariant samples; they can't always hoist conditional ones.

    3.2 Prefer branchless arithmetic over uniform branching

    GPU fragments execute in lockstep across a warp / wavefront (typically 32 or 64 lanes). When an if branches differently across lanes, both sides of the branch execute and the results are masked — the "diverged" cost is the sum, not the average.

    A branchless pattern is often cheaper:

    // DIVERGENT (per-fragment branch on a control value)
vec3 col;
if (u_audio_gate > 0.5 && bass > 0.1) {
    col = pulse_color * bass;
} else {
    col = base_color;
}

// BRANCHLESS (mix on the predicate)
float gate = step(0.5, u_audio_gate) * step(0.1, bass);
vec3 col = mix(base_color, pulse_color * bass, gate);

    mix / step / smoothstep are sampler-grade fast paths on nearly every GPU. Use them in preference to per-lane control flow whenever the predicate is cheap.

    The exception: when the branch is genuinely uniform across the warp (e.g. a toggle uniform that's the same for every fragment), GLSL compilers can collapse it to a true branch. That case is free. The expensive case is per-fragment divergence.

    3.3 iTime / chroma_time cost vs iFrame

    Both are scalar uniforms — the per-frame read cost is the same. What matters is what you do with them:

    • chroma_time (float seconds) is wallclock-aligned. Use it for animation phase, oscillators, wave functions. It is monotonically increasing within a session.
    • iFrame (integer frame counter) is useful for stochastic patterns that need to sample a different pseudo-random value per frame. Modular arithmetic on iFrame is your friend for temporal dither, accumulation, or frame-blending tricks.

    The performance impact comes from the shape of the math you build on them. A sin(chroma_time * 6.283 * freq) is one transcendental per fragment; if you need that pattern many times, compute it once into a local and reuse. A nested-trig phase function like sin(cos(sin(t * a) * b) * c) may look concise but executes three transcendentals per fragment.

    3.4 Lygia helpers vs hand-rolled math

    Chromaticity ships Lygia v1.1.4 vendored under third_party/lygia/ (per Shader Sourcing Research). Lygia is a utility library — noise, SDF, blend, palette, raymarch helpers — that composes against the chroma_* uniforms cleanly.

    When to reach for Lygia:

    • Noise functions: lygia/generative/cnoise.glsl (classic Perlin), lygia/generative/snoise.glsl (Simplex), lygia/generative/fbm.glsl (fractional Brownian motion), lygia/generative/voronoi.glsl (cellular). The vendored implementations are reference-quality ports of canonical algorithms.
    • SDF primitives: lygia/sdf/ carries sphereSDF, boxSDF, etc. Hand-rolling these is a foot-gun.
    • Colour conversions: lygia/color/ carries the standard sRGB / linear / HSL / OKLAB / palette helpers.

    When to skip Lygia and hand-roll:

    • One-shot math that's smaller than the include. A single smoothstep doesn't need a helper file.
    • Performance-critical inner loops where the call boundary cost (or per-call uniform setup, if the helper expects extra arguments) is comparable to the helper's body.
    • Anything where you need exact control over WGSL transpile semantics (Phase-2+ when the GPU pipeline lands).

    License-trial caveat. Lygia ships under Prosperity-3.0.0 with a 30-day commercial trial. The trial does not bind Chromaticity's shipped product — the license election lands at the Phase-5 EV-LICENSE-AUDIT gate. Treat Lygia as a foreground tool during authoring; pre-stage the MIT-licensed alternative (webgl-noise + glslify + three.js post-FX subsets per the Shader Sourcing Research shortlist) if you ship a scene library you intend to redistribute.

    3.5 Multipass composition costs scale with pass count

    The scene schema's PASSES array declares N render passes that compose into the final frame. Each pass:

    • Pays a framebuffer-bind cost (~tens of microseconds on a modern GPU).
    • Pays a texture-allocation cost on first use (one-time per pass).
    • Reads the previous pass's output as a sampler2D texture.

    Three passes is generally cheap. Eight is generous but manageable. Twenty is asking the operator's integrated-GPU laptop to drop frames.

    Pattern. Combine passes where the math allows it. If pass 2 does a horizontal blur and pass 3 does a vertical blur, that's a classic separable filter and is a clean two-pass design — keep it two. But if pass 4 does a colour grade that could fold into the final composite of pass 5, fold it.

    The FLOAT per-pass flag (preserved in pass extensions at slice-3a) marks a pass as needing 16- or 32-bit per-channel precision; that doubles or quadruples the framebuffer memory footprint compared to a default 8-bit-per-channel target. Use FLOAT only when you need HDR or feedback accumulation precision.

    3.6 The canvas-2d preview adapter is not your final render

    The preview tiles in Perform / Library / Author are rendered by src/lib/widgets/SceneCanvas.svelte against the 16 procedural renderers in scene-renderers.ts. This is an operator preview adapter, not a GPU rasterisation of your shader. Specifically:

    • It runs on the main UI thread via requestAnimationFrame and HTML CanvasRenderingContext2D.
    • It resolves a SceneKind from your scene's metadata (title, tags, category) and picks one of 16 procedural patterns to display.
    • It honours the three named pre-multipliers (speed, intensity, tint) plus the four mirror / invert post-pass flags described in §1.8 above.
    • It reads the live audio snapshot when present so the preview pulse responds to real audio.

    What it does not do:

    • Execute your main.glsl source.
    • Render your multipass chain.
    • Resolve lygia/ includes.
    • Respect any uniform other than the named pre-multipliers above.

    ADR-011 Phase-2+ work will replace the preview adapter with a real wgpu-backed mini renderer that does run your shader. Until then, the preview communicates "this is what the scene's about" rather than "this is what your shader literally outputs at this moment."

    Pattern. Don't tune your scene's look against the preview tile. Tune it against the live render (Perform's main canvas, Author's live-preview pane in non-preview-tile mode). Use the preview tile as a visual table-of-contents.

    3.7 Profile against the live runtime, not synthetic frames

    The EV-PERF-BUDGETS work shipped a synthetic-workload bench harness for the engine subsystems, but the only honest signal for "does my scene hit 60 Hz" is the Perform top-bar FPS readout under your actual scene workload. Two posture rules:

    • Measure with audio active. A silent-mic test underestimates the analysis-thread cost; the scheduler tick that drains the audio snapshot is part of your per-frame budget.
    • Measure with the controls you intend to ship. A control that defaults to a cheap value (intensity = 0) hides cost that the operator will pay later when they dial it up. Sweep the controls through their declared range before declaring the scene shippable.

    3.8 Cheap defaults, expensive worst cases

    A useful sanity check: the cheapest possible state of your scene (all sliders at zero, all toggles off) should hit 60 Hz on an integrated GPU. The most expensive state (all sliders at max, all toggles on) should still degrade gracefully — drop to 30 Hz, not freeze.

    If a particular control flips the scene from cheap to crash- expensive, that's a sign the control needs a clamp at the shader-author boundary, not the operator boundary. A raymarch_steps slider declared MAX = 200 is the operator choosing to render at 5 fps; declare MAX = 64 and let your shader's algorithmic budget do the right thing within that range.
